\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Marine species with high dispersal capabilities often exhibit minimal geographic population structure while maintaining significant adaptive genomic variation. In this study, we employ a chromosome-level reference genome and whole-genome data from 633 northern shortfin squid, \illex{}, collected across the Northwest Atlantic, to elucidate genomic variation previously inaccessible with a divergent reference assembly. Our findings confirm nearly complete range-wide panmixia, yet reveal a singular major axis of genomic structure: a $\sim$19~Mb inversion on chromosome~2. This derived arrangement segregates at a frequency of 0.374, is present throughout the species' range without a geographic cline, and achieves $\Fst=0.37$ between alternative homozygotes despite a geographic $\Fst\approx0$. Its evolutionary trajectory is inconsistent with neutral drift. The rarer derived arrangement retains 2.3 times more diversity than expected given its current frequency, and barrier-aware coalescent modeling dates the polymorphism to approximately 800--850~ka, indicating a prolonged period near frequency $p\approx0.70$--0.74 followed by a rapid decline to its present frequency that ended at least $\sim$100~ka ago. The inversion is gene-poor and lacks functional enrichment, yet contains thousands of near-fixed differences concentrated within the non-recombining block, suggesting selection on the arrangement as a long-lived supergene rather than on a single locally adaptive locus. Outside the inversion, demographic analyses indicate strong expansion (genome-wide Tajima's $D\approx-2$) to a current effective population size of approximately seven million. A stratified scan, corroborated across methods, for recent positive selection identified 627 candidate sweep windows, including 24 high-confidence regions independently supported by multiple methods. Genes associated with these sweeps show significant enrichment for neurodevelopment and photoreception, with additional candidates involving lineage-specific genes and Z-linked RNA-regulatory and hormone-signaling loci. Collectively, these findings reveal two contrasting modes of adaptation in an otherwise panmictic marine species: a long-lived chromosomal polymorphism maintained by balancing selection and recent directional selection on sensory and neural functions. More broadly, they illustrate how a conspecific reference genome can uncover structural and adaptive variation that remains largely undetectable through geographic differentiation alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Marine species with vast census sizes and high dispersal pose a distinctive problem for population genomics. Weak geographic differentiation is exactly what population-genetic theory predicts for such species, because migration homogenizes allele frequencies faster than drift can differentiate them, and a large effective size makes selection efficient while slowing drift. The difficulty is one of detection rather than expectation. The standard tools of population genomics, including geographic $\Fst$, principal-component clustering, and outlier scans among sampling sites, all rely on differentiation among locations, so structural and adaptive variation that does not track geography can remain undetected in precisely the species where selection should be most effective.

Chromosomal inversions are a prime example of such variation. Inversions inhibit recombination between alternative arrangements, thereby maintaining combinations of alleles as segregating ``supergenes'' \citep{kirkpatrick2010,schwander2014,thompson2014,wellenreuther2018,faria2019}, and their evolutionary significance arises not only from their breakpoints but also from the long haplotypes they preserve. Numerous well-documented instances exhibit noticeable phenotypic effects or occur in spatially structured populations, such as mimicry in \textit{Heliconius} \citep{jay2021}, alternative reproductive morphs in the ruff \citep{lamichhaney2016}, colony organization in fire ants \citep{wang2013}, and life-history variation in \textit{Mimulus} \citep{lowry2010}. Consequently, much of the inversion theory has concentrated on local adaptation with migration, where suppressed recombination safeguards locally advantageous allele combinations from gene flow \citep{kirkpatrick2006,charlesworth2018inv}. That explanation has the least scope in a large, weakly structured population, so an inversion maintained there at intermediate frequency without geographic structuring presents a distinct challenge. Its persistence suggests balancing mechanisms such as overdominance, associative overdominance induced by recessive deleterious load \citep{zhao2016,gilbert2020,jay2021}, negative frequency dependence, or temporally fluctuating selection \citep{wittmann2017,rudman2022}, and because large effective size slows drift, the history of such a polymorphism can extend over hundreds of thousands of generations. Differentiating these histories from neutral drift necessitates reconstructing the age and frequency history of the arrangements while explicitly considering the recombination barrier between them \citep{navarro2000,guerrero2012,berdan2023}.

Pelagic cephalopods exemplify both problems. Ommastrephid squids, which are short-lived, semelparous, highly fecund, and capable of extensive dispersal during both early life stages and adulthood, generate large and often highly variable populations whose abundance is closely linked to oceanographic conditions \citep{odor1998,arkhipkin2015}. The northern shortfin squid, \illex{}, exemplifies this life history. It is an annual semelparous broadcast spawner distributed over more than 2{,}500~km of the Northwest Atlantic shelf and slope, from the Mid-Atlantic Bight to Newfoundland and Labrador, where it supports major commercial fisheries in both U.S. and Canadian waters \citep{odor1998,arkhipkin2015,baker2025}. Recruitment and distribution are strongly influenced by transport in the Gulf Stream and associated shelf and slope circulation, while adults undertake extensive horizontal and diel vertical migrations. Collectively, high dispersal, extreme fecundity, and large population size predict minimal persistent geographic structure, while providing a substantial substrate for natural selection. The same characteristics complicate genomic inference. The fecundity-with-high-mortality demography of \illex{} motivates the use of sweepstakes-reproductive-success models, wherein a small and variable fraction of adults contributes disproportionately to each generation \citep{hedgecock2011,eldon2006,arnason2023}. A large effective population size can simultaneously enhance the efficacy of weak selection, maintain abundant standing variation, and result in very short linkage disequilibrium, thereby complicating the disentanglement of population history and recent selection. More broadly, genomic analysis of cephalopods has lagged behind that of many commercially important vertebrates due to their large, repeat-rich, and extensively reorganized genomes relative to other molluscs \citep{albertin2015,albertin2022,schmidbaur2022,coffing2025,coffing2026}. Until recently, these limitations were particularly pronounced for \illex{}, as no conspecific chromosome-level reference genome was available.

The inaugural comprehensive population genomic analysis of \illex{} established a crucial baseline. Utilizing whole-genome data from 540 specimens collected from over 40 locations, \citet{baker2025} identified an absence of geographic genetic structure across the species' Northwest Atlantic range. This finding aligns with the hypothesis of extensive connectivity facilitated by early-life dispersal and adult migration, thereby supporting the classification of \illex{} as a single, highly interconnected stock. Nevertheless, due to the lack of a conspecific genome assembly, the reads were aligned to the chromosome-level genome of the distantly related diamondback squid, \textit{Thysanoteuthis rhombus}, which diverged approximately 51 million years ago, resulting in a mean alignment rate of merely $\sim$46\%. This divergence inherently limits inference to the conserved genome fraction that can be reliably mapped, thereby diminishing the ability to detect lineage-specific sequences, structural polymorphisms, and rapidly evolving loci. Reference divergence matters most for inversions. Detecting an inversion requires mapping both its breakpoints and the long differentiated haplotypes between them, and both are lost where reads map poorly, so a population studied against a distant reference can appear to lack inversions it actually carries. Consistent with this, Baker et al. did not find evidence of large structural variants and explicitly acknowledged reference divergence as a constraint on their detection.

A chromosome-level \illex{} genome facilitates the investigation of selection beyond large structural polymorphisms. Cephalopods present a unique context for this inquiry because of their extreme life history, which is associated with remarkable sensory and neural complexity. Coleoid genomes demonstrate significant lineage-specific reorganization \citep{schmidbaur2022}, extensive RNA-editing-based transcriptomic plasticity \citep{liscovitch2017}, and sophisticated nervous and visual systems \citep{albertin2015,albertin2022,styfhals2022,songcocasey2022}. In squids, vision adapts to substantial changes in depth and illumination during diel vertical migration, with photoreception extending beyond the camera eye to include dermal opsins and light-responsive chromatophore systems \citep{mathger2010,ramirez2015,kingston2015}. In a large, highly connected population where geographic $\Fst$ offers limited utility for detecting adaptation, recent positive selection is likely to manifest primarily through local distortions in diversity, linkage disequilibrium, and the site-frequency spectrum. Accurate interpretation of these signals necessitates first establishing the demographic and genealogical context in which they emerged.

In this study, we employed a chromosome-level annotated reference genome for \illex{}, recently made available \citep{small2026}, to reanalyze range-wide whole-genome variation with a significantly enhanced genomic resolution. Our dataset included 633 individuals distributed across 11 fishery management divisions \citep{baker2025}, along with \coin{} \citep{coindetii2025} as an outgroup for divergence-based inference and \textit{I.\ argentinus} as a recently diverged species \citep{small2026}. Initially, we assessed whether the range-wide panmixia reported by \citet{baker2025} persisted when reads were aligned to a conspecific reference and investigated the emergence of previously inaccessible structural variations. This analysis identified a single $\sim$19~Mb inversion on chromosome~2 that segregated across the range, despite an otherwise largely unstructured genome. Subsequently, we determined whether this inversion represented a neutral polymorphism or a selected supergene by integrating arrangement-specific diversity and divergence with a barrier-aware coalescent model to reconstruct its age and historical frequency trajectories. Concurrently, we inferred population history from the site frequency spectrum and linkage disequilibrium, explicitly evaluating whether the data necessitated a multiple-merger rather than Kingman coalescent, and scanned the remaining genome for recent positive selection using complementary methods \citep{kern2018diploshic,alachiotis2018raisd,degiorgio2016}. These analyses elucidated two distinct forms of genomic adaptation in an otherwise panmictic species: a long-lived, balancing-selected chromosomal polymorphism and a genome-wide set of recent sweeps concentrated on neurodevelopmental and photoreceptive functions. Collectively, these studies demonstrated how a conspecific genome transformed the apparent genomic landscape of a highly connected marine species, revealing structural and adaptive variations that were largely undetectable through geographic differentiation alone.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Sample, sex, and the absence of geographic structure}
We conducted an analysis of 633 \illex{} individuals from eleven NAFO management divisions, covering approximately 37--50$^\circ$N (\cref{fig:map}, \cref{tab:summary}). Details of each collection event, including source and depth, are provided in Supplementary Table~S8. The read coverage on the Z chromosome categorizes the sample into 342 ZZ males and 291 ZO females, aligning with a ZZ/ZO system \citep{coffing2025} (\cref{fig:overview}). Autosomal analyses incorporate both sexes, while the Z chromosome, where males are diploid and females are haploid, is examined separately (\cref{sec:sweep}).

Consistent with range-wide panmixia reported by \citet{baker2025}, population structure is observed in a specific genomic region, although it is not geographically linked. A windowed principal-component analysis along chromosome~2 identifies the three-cluster structure within the inversion (among-cluster variance $\eta^{2}=0.748$ within the inversion compared to $\eta^{2}=0.003$ in the collinear sequence, Supplementary Fig.~S3), and two genome-wide tests confirm its uniqueness. First, the chromosome-2 karyotype does not account for variation on any other chromosome (in a per-chromosome PCA colored by karyotype, $\eta^{2}=0.76$ on chr2 versus $\leq0.01$ on all 45 others). Second, independent of the chr2 labels, a Gaussian-mixture screen of each chromosome's leading principal component supports more than one cluster only on chromosome~2 (three clusters, $\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=199$, cluster separation $8.6\sigma$) and on the Z chromosome (two clusters, reflecting the expected ZZ/ZO hemizygosity split), while every autosome forms a single cloud (\cref{fig:overview}). The residual dispersion on minor PCA axes does not correlate with division, latitude, or coverage (Supplementary Fig.~S1), and a KING-robust relatedness screen \citep{manichaikul2010} identifies no duplicate, parent-offspring, or full-sib pairs, and only a single second-degree pair among the 633 individuals (maximum kinship 0.16), indicating effective unrelatedness. Given that a per-SNP differentiation scan is the most sensitive test for local adaptation in such a panmictic population, we compared per-SNP $\Fst$ among divisions to a coalescent-neutral null, simulating a single panmictic population under the fitted demography at the empirical sample sizes and missingness. Across the complete per-chromosome callsets of all 633 individuals, thinned to every fiftieth SNP for tractability (551{,}344 autosomal SNPs, those in genes covering 12{,}007 genes), the empirical differentiation remains entirely within the drift expectation. No SNP surpasses the neutral maximum, only a single intergenic site exceeds the lenient threshold against approximately 556 expected by chance, and there are no genic outliers (Supplementary Figs.~S2, S19). Nucleotide diversity and Tajima's $D$ are consistent across latitude, and pairwise $\Fst$ among divisions is approximately 0 throughout (\cref{fig:diversity}). Consequently, there is no geographic local adaptation anywhere in the genome, and the inversion itself is geographically non-clinal (\cref{fig:map}, Supplementary Fig.~S6 and Table~S2). Furthermore, arrangement frequency does not correlate with capture depth, which ranges from 3 to 613~m across collection events (Supplementary Table~S8). Among 539 individuals with recorded depth, the correlation between depth and the number of inverted chromosomes carried is $\rho=-0.08$ ($p=0.08$), and within the six divisions sampled at multiple depths, it is $\rho=+0.04$ (within-division permutation $p=0.49$). A weak negative slope across collection events ($p=0.04$) is entirely attributable to the two shallow, jig-caught Newfoundland (3 K) samples and disappears when they are excluded ($p=0.23$), indicating that it cannot be disentangled from gear and location effects.

\subsection{A large, balancing-selected inversion on chromosome~2}\label{sec:inv}

\paragraph{Identity, breakpoints, and karyotypes.}
Local PCA \citep{li2019lostruct} and a Gaussian-mixture karyotype assignment
(95.3\% confident) resolve three genotype clusters on chromosome~2 (254 AA, 284 AB, and 95 BB individuals, \cref{fig:inv}a) whose differentiation is karyotypic
rather than geographic, with $\Fst(\mathrm{AA,BB})=0.37$ inside the inversion
against a geographic $\Fst\approx0$. Between-karyotype $\Fst$ computed along the
whole chromosome is $\approx0$ (median 0.006) across collinear sequence and jumps
to a median of 0.34 (maximum 0.53) inside the inversion (Supplementary
Fig.~S20), so the karyotypes are one panmictic population everywhere except
within the barrier. The three karyotypes co-occur throughout the range with no
latitudinal cline in arrangement frequency (0.29--0.46 across divisions, \cref{fig:map}, Supplementary Fig.~S6 and Table~S2). Two independent lines pin
the breakpoints to chr2:60.54--79.50~Mb (a $\sim$18.96~Mb inversion),
namely a step-change in arrangement differentiation and a strand flip in a
whole-chromosome alignment to \coin{} (Supplementary Fig.~S11), and an extended block of linkage disequilibrium coincides with the same interval (Supplementary
Fig.~S4).

\paragraph{The reference genome carries the derived arrangement.} The derived,
inverted arrangement is BB, at a present frequency of 0.374, and AA is the ancestral, standard arrangement. This is the reverse of the naive polarization from the reference
allele, which is circular because the \illex{} reference assembly is itself in
the inverted orientation (it carries BB at 86.5\% of fixed-different sites).
Restricting to the non-circular subset of sites at which \coin{} carries the
\illex{} alternate allele shows AA to be ancestral (63.0\%) and BB derived.
There was a single inversion event, and the reference was assembled from an
inverted individual (Supplementary Table~S3).

\paragraph{The inversion is non-neutral.}
The primary evidence is per-arrangement diversity (\cref{tab:inv},
\cref{fig:inv}c). Inside an inversion the two arrangements behave as partially
isolated demes \citep{navarro2000,guerrero2012}, and without gene flux between them the
neutral diversity of each scales with its frequency \citep{navarro2000,peischl2013}. The rare derived arrangement is
instead more diverse than its frequency can sustain, with
$\pi_I/\pi_S=1.356\pm0.048$ against an equilibrium expectation of $p/(1-p)=0.60$
for a polymorphism held at 0.374, a 2.3-fold excess that is the signature of an
old polymorphism that was once common, not of a recent rise. Two diffusion
arguments that use neither the mutation rate nor the accessibility mask exclude
neutrality (\cref{sec:methods}). The first concerns attainment. A neutral allele
must first reach the inferred peak frequency of 0.70--0.74 before it can decline,
and the expected time for drift to carry a new mutation to that frequency is
1.9--2.1~$\Ne$ generations, which is at least 1.06 million generations even at the
smallest $\Ne$ on the trajectory (the ancestral $5.5\times10^{5}$), 1.25 to 1.44 times
the entire inferred lifetime of the inversion, and up to 18 times at the present $\Ne$. The probability that a new neutral inversion ever reaches 0.70 is of order
$10^{-6}$. The second concerns the decline. Over a 100-ky fall, the middle of the fitted range, at the
$\Ne$ prevailing then ($2.8$--$3.8\times10^{6}$), the standard deviation of allele
frequency under pure drift is 0.055, so the observed fall of 0.366 is 6.7 standard deviations of drift. Even a fall four times slower would remain 2.5
standard deviations away, and a coalescent profile of the fall duration excludes
the slow, drift-compatible alternative at $\Delta\chi^{2}\gtrsim8$--80
(Supplementary Fig.~S14). Both arguments bound drift alone and take the fitted
peak frequency as given, but together they establish that the arrangement was
neither carried up nor brought down by drift.

\paragraph{Age and trajectory of the inversion.}
Estimating an inversion's age requires a coalescent in which the two
arrangements exchange ancestry only across the barrier, which standard simulators do not provide. We therefore fit the inversion's age and frequency trajectory
with msinv \citep{msinv}, a sequentially Markov coalescent simulator with recombination that
enforces the cross-karyotype recombination barrier, tracks ancestral material,
and outputs tree sequences (\cref{sec:methods}). The data constrain the shape of
the frequency trajectory $p(t)$ rather than any site-level mechanism, so we
condition on a deterministic trajectory under within-arrangement neutrality and
fit it by grid search to $\pi_I/\pi_S$, $\dxy/\pi_I$, and the per-arrangement singleton
fraction of the site-frequency spectrum (\cref{fig:demog}b, Supplementary Fig.~S14, Supplementary Methods). The coalescent age is
$\sim$800--850~ky. It is the robustly determined quantity because it is carried by
$\dxy$ between arrangements, which is symmetric to the polarization. The
reconstructed history is that the inversion arose $\sim$800--850~ka, coincident with
the onset of the population expansion (\cref{sec:demog}), was maintained at $p\approx0.70$--0.74 for most of its history, and then fell sharply to 0.374 over
50--200~ky, in a fall that ended at least $\sim$100~ka ago, under selection of order
$\Ne s\sim10^{2}$--$10^{3}$ ($s\approx0.7$--$3\times10^{-4}$). The fits used a 29.7-kb simulated inversion, and the
fitted statistics change by less than 5\% when extrapolated from that length to the 19-Mb inversion (Supplementary Methods).
The observed joint two-dimensional site-frequency spectrum between
arrangements shows the same barrier directly, with near-fixed differences and off-diagonal density in the
inversion and a collapse to the diagonal in collinear controls
(Supplementary Fig.~S5). Every absolute age scales with the assumed
mutation rate $\mu=3\times10^{-9}$. 

\paragraph{Inversion as a balanced supergene.} 
Recombination across the inversion is best read from linkage disequilibrium 
rather than from the recombination map. The map was estimated from all karyotypes 
together and from linkage disequilibrium within $\sim$19-kb windows, whereas 
the barrier acts only between arrangements and across the whole 19-Mb block. 
Within each window the many SNPs that segregate within arrangements dominate the 
signal, and the between-arrangement association carried by the few near-fixed 
differences is spread across megabases, so the map cannot resolve the barrier. 
We therefore report the map only for the collinear part of chromosome~2, which 
recombines at an ordinary autosomal rate (median 0.202 against an autosomal median of 0.200~cM/Mb, Supplementary Fig.~S12). The barrier itself is shown 
directly by the block of linkage disequilibrium that spans exactly the 
inversion (Supplementary Fig.~S4), by the arrangement differentiation, and 
by the joint site-frequency spectrum (\cref{fig:inv}b, Supplementary Fig.~S5). It is not locally adaptive, since there is
no cline and geographic $\Fst\approx0$, and the migration-selection scenario that
explains most well-studied inversions \citep{kirkpatrick2006} cannot apply. And
it is not a swept region, because the genome-wide scan below finds no
cross-method-concordant sweep inside the inversion. The RAiSD footprint
statistic is broadly elevated and the classifier over-calls across the
inversion, but the two never colocalize into focal concordance, as expected
for a balancing-selected supergene rather than a swept locus, and the
concordance requirement filters these calls correctly. Gene flux between
arrangements is not detectable in mean $\dxy$, though a residual $\sim$40\%
depletion of near-fixed differences appears in the outermost megabase around the
breakpoints. The mechanism of balancing selection, i.e., overdominance,
associative overdominance, frequency dependence, or a moving optimum under
fluctuating selection, is not resolved by these data. Though probable that a
shelf-spawning squid whose paralarvae ride the Gulf Stream would experience
temporally fluctuating selection on $\sim$100~ky glacial cycles, however this
remains a hypothesis.

\paragraph{Gene content and the architecture of arrangement divergence.}
The inversion has no functionally significant enrichment. Its 89 gene models (4.7 per Mb, against 6.8 per Mb genome-wide) 
place it at the 15th percentile of autosomal windows of the same size (median 127 genes, interquartile range 100--151), 
and the same holds for annotated content, since 45 genes have a cross-species functional annotation and 29 carry 
gene-ontology terms, again the 15th percentile (median 49 GO-annotated genes per window). Their functional classes 
are diverse (lipid metabolism, DNA repair, transcription and splicing, signalling) with no coherent pathway 
(Supplementary Table~S7). A gene-ontology test of its annotated genes against the genome
returns no enriched terms at FDR$<$0.05, and a permutation null shows this is not a power artifact, 
because random gene sets of the same size produce more nominal hits than the inversion genes (86.6$\pm$60 against 69, permutation $p=0.51$),
while the largest nominal per-term enrichments ($\sim$40-fold) rest on as few as two genes (Supplementary Fig.~S21).
The inversion has thus captured a functionally unremarkable slice of the genome,
not a selected gene set but a supergene maintained for the arrangement as a whole. What the arrangements do 
accumulate is divergence as a unit, with 3{,}513 near-fixed differences between AA and BB
(per-arrangement $|\Delta\mathrm{AF}|\geq0.9$) are spread across the inversion (Supplementary Fig.~S5). These are
overwhelmingly non-coding since coding sequence is only $\sim$0.2\% of the region, but the exonic fixed
differences are $\sim$2.3-fold over-represented and clustered. All 19 fall in just nine genes, led by a 
cytochrome~P450 (\textit{CYP4X1}) and an acidic-repeat protein (four each) and including a phospholipase~A2, 
the ciliary gene \textit{RPGRIP1L}, and a cysteine-rich venom-like protein.
Among coding sites inside the inversion, the most differentiated between arrangements are depleted 
rather than enriched for nonsynonymous (zero-fold degenerate) positions. Of the top 1\% of 
coding sites by between-arrangement $\Fst$, 45\% are zero-fold, against 82\% of all coding sites in the inversion 
(odds ratio 0.18, $p=2\times10^{-34}$), and per-transcript divergence between arrangements is unremarkable 
relative to collinear controls (5 of 95 transcripts exceed the control 95th percentile, as expected by chance, Supplementary Methods). 
Purifying selection therefore still removes deleterious differences between the arrangements, and any recessive 
load carried by the rarer arrangement is not yet visible as excess nonsynonymous divergence.

\subsection{Demographic history shows an expanding population}\label{sec:demog}
The age and selection estimates above depend on the population's demography and on the coalescent class
being Kingman. Genome-wide, \illex{} shows $\pi\approx0.0093$ and Tajima's $D\approx-2$ in essentially every
window \citep{tajima1989,tajima1989b}, an unambiguous, genome-wide signature of expansion (\cref{tab:demog},
\cref{fig:demog}a, \cref{fig:diversity}). A moments fit to the folded SFS gives growth from an ancestral
$\Ne=$ 547{,}928 to a present $\Ne=$ 6{,}808{,}096 over 769{,}519 generations ($\sim$12-fold, with generations equal to years in this annual species), and this history reproduces both the observed $\pi$ and a full-SFS
Tajima's $D=-1.92$ under forward simulation. Stairway~Plot~2 and GONE2 concur on strong recent expansion to
$\Ne$ in the $1.5$--$1.7\times10^{7}$ range. Because sweepstakes reproduction would mimic expansion by
inflating rare variants \citep{eldon2006,hedgecock2011,eldon2015}, we tested the coalescent class directly. 
The folded SFS is fit far better by a Kingman-plus-growth model than by any multiple-merger (Beta) coalescent
\citep{schweinsberg2003} (shape deviation $L_1=0.036$ vs a best-$\alpha$ 0.108), and contemporary $\Ne$
estimates from the SFS and from linkage disequilibrium agree where both are inconsistent with a strong
multiple-merger process. The expansion began $\sim$770~ka, i.e.\ at essentially the same time as the
inversion arose, which places the supergene's origin at the start of the demographic episode that shapes the
whole genome.

\subsection{Recombination, background selection, and protein adaptation}
Three further features of the genome were resolved to accurately identify selective sweeps. Recombination 
was estimated with ReLERNN \citep{adrion2020relernn}, a deep-learning method that infers the rate from linkage disequilibrium. The estimated rate is $\sim$0.20~cM/Mb on the 
autosomes (median 0.200, Supplementary Fig.~S12). The map is flat with respect to gene content, 
and the rate varies by only $\sim$6\% between windows adjacent to exons and windows $>$100~kb from any exon, unchanged 
when $\pi$ is held fixed (Supplementary Fig.~S17a). For example, on chromosome~1 the rolling median spans just 0.17--0.23~cM/Mb, 
the pericentromeric flanks where $\pi$ halves show no more than a 5\% dip, and the centromere itself 
(a 2.5-Mb block that is 80\% repeat and almost entirely masked) carries no windows (Supplementary Fig.~S22). 
This pattern is similar genome-wide. Across all 45 scanned autosomes the chromosome-scale rolling median 
varies by only 10--41\% of each chromosome's median, and no chromosome shows a peak more than 25\% above it. 
Background selection is moderate and gene-density-driven. Nucleotide diversity falls with local coding density
($r=-0.31$, a 20--30\% reduction in gene-dense windows) and rises 43\% between exon-adjacent and exon-distant
windows (Supplementary Fig.~S17) \citep{charlesworth1993,mcvicker2009,charlesworth2021}. A model-based 
$B$-map could not be fit under this regime (the classical approximation overflows where the deleterious 
mutation rate exceeds the selection coefficient), so local coding fraction serves as the background-selection 
covariate that the sweep scan controls for explicitly. Protein adaptation over the divergence timescale
is substantial. McDonald--Kreitman tests \citep{mcdonald1991,messer2013mk} against \coin{} across 20{,}999 genes give a
genome-wide proportion of adaptive substitutions $\alpha\approx0.34$--0.64, with the splicing factor
\textit{SF3B4} the single most significantly adaptive gene (Supplementary Fig.~S10). Per-gene power is limited,
so we treat the MK results as complementary to the recent-sweep scan, which detects a different timescale of 
selection, recent and often non-coding. \textit{I.\ argentinus} is a striking comparator. 
It is recently diverged (an order of magnitude fewer fixed coding differences than \coin{}, Supplementary Table~S3) 
yet carries near-identical genome-wide diversity ($\pi=0.0092$ vs 0.0093, within 1\%), so the two are large-$\Ne$, 
expansion-shaped congeners of matched standing variation.
We also found twenty-two blocks of $\geq$1~Mb on 17 chromosomes (129~Mb, 3.9\% of the autosomes) that seemingly 
carry 2--6 times the chromosome-median $\pi$ with less negative Tajima's $D$ (Supplementary Table~S9). 
They are present at the same strength in every sampling location, are no more differentiated among locations
than flanking sequence, and lack the long-range linkage disequilibrium of a segregating inversion (Supplementary Figs.~S23, S24).
Read-depth analyses resolve them into three categories. In six, depth is raised in every individual
(1.2--2.2 times the flanks across the population sample, and up to 2.7 and 2.4 times in long reads 
from the individual used for the assembly and a second individual \citep{small2026}), and a single diploid genome carries 
five to six times the flanking density of heterozygous sites at skewed allele fractions. We interpret this as a duplicated 
sequence collapsed into one copy in the assembly. In eight, short-read depth is reduced to 0.6--0.9 times the flanks in every individual. A third of their accessible sequence is not uniquely mappable by 150-bp reads, against 4\% genome-wide, so these blocks are largely duplicated within the assembly itself and short reads there map to several places and are lost at the mapping-quality filters. Long reads, which map uniquely through the duplicated sequence, show a much smaller deficit, and divergent haplotypes may add to the loss. The remaining eight have normal depth and mapping and are gene-poor regions of genuinely 
elevated diversity. Since our summary statistics cannot separate any of these from the footprints of selection, 
all 22 blocks were excluded from the sweep scan and the gene-set analyses.

\subsection{A genome-wide scan for selective sweeps}\label{sec:sweep}
We scanned for recent positive selection with diploS/HIC \citep{schrider2016,kern2018diploshic}, a five-class
site-frequency/diversity-based classifier (neutral, hard, soft, linked-hard, and linked-soft, in 1.1~Mb feature windows of eleven 100~kb sub-windows) trained on forward and coalescent simulations under the fitted growth
demography with the final model reaching 81\% class accuracy (\cref{fig:selection}, Supplementary Figs.~S7 and S8).
Applied to the genome, the classifier over-calls sweeps, assigning only 1\% of windows to the neutral class and 91\% to the linked-hard or linked-soft classes.
We determined this was because real neutral windows (accessible, $\geq$5~kb from any gene) carry 3--8-fold more
within-window spatial heterogeneity in $\pi$ and $\theta$ than the simulations, even though their marginal
SFS matches ($D\approx-2$). We went further and simulated our identical missing-data structure and sample incompleteness 
ultimately ruling that out as the cause. The residual is real per-sub-window diversity variation (mutation-rate variation plus background selection)
that a uniform-$\theta$ coalescent does not reproduce \citep{johri2022}. We therefore use the classifier as a stratified empirical-outlier detector,
in which the sweep score ($p_\mathrm{hard}+p_\mathrm{soft}$) is ranked within strata of callable fraction and coding density,
so a window is an outlier only relative to windows of similar accessibility and gene density, and calls are corroborated by an
independent LD-footprint method, RAiSD \citep{alachiotis2018raisd}, or a composite-likelihood SFS method, SweepFinder2 \citep{nielsen2005,degiorgio2016}.
Thus, we report sweeps at two levels of stringency (Supplementary Fig.~S13). Tier~1 is the diploS/HIC hard/soft classification,
spatially resolved by a recombination-aware Markov decoder, a hidden Markov model over the per-window (100~kb) classes with neutral,
linked, and sweep-centre states whose emissions are the prior-corrected class posteriors and whose dwell lengths
follow the $\sim$400~kb sweep footprint scaled by the recombination map (Supplementary Fig.~S13a). Because the genome
reads as $\sim$91\% linked-selected under strong expansion, the decoder's role is spatial coherence.
It retains hard/soft calls that lie in contiguous sweep centers and discards spatially isolated ones,
reducing 1{,}651 raw calls to 677 windows (623 localized autosomal calls, 437 hard and 186 soft) and preferentially
keeping the higher-outlier windows (median empirical-outlier percentile 0.98 vs 0.95 for discarded calls, Supplementary Fig.~S13b).
Tier~2 is the subset independently corroborated by a other methods, producing 24 concordant regions (a diploS/HIC-HMM
outlier plus RAiSD or SweepFinder2). The two tiers agree strongly, with 88\% of windows at concordant loci decoded as sweep centers 
versus 2.9\% genome-wide (odds ratio 242, $p<10^{-30}$), and 21 of the 24 concordant
regions contain a Tier-1 center (Supplementary Fig.~S13c), so the conservative concordance set is a
high-purity subset of the classifier calls, not a disjoint list. The Z chromosome is necessarily diploS/HIC-only
and scanned separately in males (below), it contributes four further Tier-1 calls, for a genome-wide 
Tier-1 total of 627 (\cref{fig:selection}, Supplementary Fig.~S18).

\paragraph{Neurodevelopment and photoreception.}
The 623 autosomal Tier-1 calls overlap 885 genes (475 with GO annotation). One-third of calls fall in
gene-poor, often transposon-rich windows and are excluded from enrichment analyses. A gene-ontology enrichment against the
annotated-gene background (Fisher's exact test with Benjamini--Hochberg FDR, \citealt{benjamini1995}) returns
124 terms at FDR$<$0.05 (Supplementary Fig.~S15, Table~S4), dominated by neural development 
(neurogenesis, generation of neurons, neuron development, neuron-projection and cell morphogenesis, at 1.5--1.8-fold), 
together with transcriptional and RNA-regulatory control, and a weaker light-response signal
(response to light stimulus 1.9-fold, response to radiation 1.8-fold). The enrichment is carried by the hard-sweep calls (98 terms), and the disjoint soft-sweep set has none at FDR$<$0.05. 
We further tested there regions aginst a hypothesis of background selection, since gene-dense regions have lower 
diversity for reasons unrelated to sweeps. We therefore screened every Tier-1 call with the same per-region
background-selection test used for the concordant set, retaining only calls whose local $\pi$ falls below the
background-selection expectation given flanking coding density. This keeps 291 of the 623 (237 hard, 54
soft). The BGS-robust set yields 151 terms at FDR$<$0.05, and every leading term's fold enrichment rises over the previous set, 
including response to light stimulus $1.94\!\to\!3.15$ (FDR $2\times10^{-2}\!\to\!9\times10^{-5}$),
response to radiation $1.76\!\to\!2.79$ (FDR $3\times10^{-2}\!\to\!2\times10^{-4}$),
neuron-projection morphogenesis $1.81\!\to\!2.25$, cell morphogenesis $1.73\!\to\!2.24$,
neuron development $1.59\!\to\!1.97$, generation of neurons $1.59\!\to\!1.90$,
transcription by RNA polymerase~II $1.73\!\to\!2.08$ (Supplementary Fig.~S15).
The photoreception signal, the weakest in the full set, becomes the single most enriched theme after the BGS screen,
and its more specific child terms (detection of light stimulus $2.5\!\to\!3.9$, phototransduction
$2.5\!\to\!3.4$) move in the same direction, though with too few genes to reach significance. A shared-gene
enrichment map of the BGS-robust set resolves the same two connected modules, neural
development/morphogenesis and light response, with no third (Supplementary Fig.~S16, Table~S5).

\paragraph{Candidate sweeps.}
The Tier-2 concordant regions carry a biologically coherent set of cephalopod-orthologous genes that sit
squarely within these themes (\cref{tab:sweep}, Supplementary Table~S6). They include the neural/EMT transcription factor
\textit{ZEB2}, the synaptic-vesicle GTPase \textit{Rab3}, the apelin receptor \textit{Aplnr}
(cardiovascular/vasculature signalling), the DNA-damage-response gene \textit{MACROD2}, the phospholipid
synthase \textit{cept1}, and the ceramide phosphoethanolamine synthase \textit{Cpes}. A per-region background-selection test isolates
11 of these as high-confidence candidates in which diversity is reduced beyond the background-selection expectation,
and fine-scale localization confirms all 11 as recent sweeps by the sign of Tajima's $D$ at the diversity valley. It reassigns two apparent gene-desert signals to transposable-element regions (flagged lower confidence) and moves two targets to neighboring genes, from \textit{eif3h} to \textit{Cpes} at chr1:85.5~Mb and to \textit{GALT} at chr25:65.0~Mb. Both are genes already in the annotation, not new gene models. The single most distinctive autosomal candidate is a cephalopod-lineage-specific gene
under a hard sweep on chromosome~16, a tandem-exon transcript with no functional annotation whose translation matches
an uncharacterised protein family in the bobtail squid \textit{Euprymna} ($E=2\times10^{-53}$) \citep{albertin2015,albertin2022}.
There is no overlap between the sweep candidates and the MK-adaptive genes.

\paragraph{The Z chromosome.}
Because males are Z-diploid and females Z-haploid, the Z was scanned separately in the 330 confident ZZ
males, using a model retrained at the matched sample size and accounting for the Z's reduced effective size.
The male-only screen was validated against the 289 haploid females, whose per-window diversity tracks the males' at $r=0.996$.
Of the four Z Tier-1 calls (three hard and one soft, Supplementary Fig.~S18), three credible, gene-localised sweeps survive
fine-scale triage, all confirmed as conserved cephalopod genes by direct homology. These are \textit{Pasilla}/Nova-1 (a neuronal alternative-splicing regulator with 92\% identity to its \textit{Octopus} ortholog),
\textit{Smaug}/samd4a (a germline RNA-binding translational repressor), and \textit{Esrrg} (an estrogen-related receptor with an adjacent likely \textit{cis}-regulatory lncRNA, with 97.5\% identity to its \textit{Octopus} ortholog),
a coherent RNA-regulation-and-hormone-signalling signature on the sex chromosome.
The \textit{Amnionless}/``Zfest'' sex-determination locus falls just outside the swept valley and 
shows only purifying selection, so it is a linked passenger rather than the target.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{The inversion is a datable, declining, balancing-selected supergene.}
Chromosomal inversions are often maintained by local adaptation in the presence of gene flow, producing geographic clines in arrangement frequency \citep{kirkpatrick2006,lowry2010,faria2019}. That explanation does not fit \illex{}. The chromosome-2 inversion is distributed throughout the species' range, its frequency is non-clinal, and geographic differentiation is effectively absent. Instead, the inversion appears to represent a long-lived balanced polymorphism maintained at the level of the arrangement. The derived arrangement (BB), although presently rarer, retains substantially more diversity than expected from its current frequency, indicating that it was historically more common. Two mutation-rate-independent arguments, together with a barrier-aware coalescent model, support an age of approximately 800{,}000--850{,}000 years and a history in which the derived arrangement remained near a frequency of 0.70--0.74 for much of its lifetime before declining to 0.37 in a rapid fall that ended at least 100{,}000 years ago.

This history differs from that of classic \textit{Drosophila} inversions, whose frequencies often vary across geographic or seasonal gradients \citep{corbettdetig2012,kapun2019}, and from the human 17q21 inversion, which has undergone a more recent increase associated with positive selection \citep{stefansson2005}. One possible analogue is the mimicry supergene of \textit{Heliconius numata}, in which an inverted arrangement carries recessive deleterious variation that contributes to associative overdominance and limits its frequency \citep{jay2021}. Associative overdominance is therefore one plausible mechanism in \illex{}, particularly because large non-recombining regions can accumulate recessive load \citep{zhao2016,gilbert2020,berdan2021}. The inversion contains clustered coding divergence, including 19 fixed exonic differences across nine genes, providing potential substrate for such a load, although the differentiated coding sites are not enriched for nonsynonymous changes. Other mechanisms remain possible. A prolonged plateau followed by decline could also arise under a changing selective optimum \citep{matuszewski2015}, and temporally fluctuating selection can maintain polymorphism over long periods in natural populations \citep{wittmann2017,rudman2022}. For a shelf-spawning squid whose paralarvae are transported by the Gulf Stream, repeated environmental change across glacial cycles provides a plausible ecological context, but this remains speculative.

The data therefore resolve the historical trajectory more clearly than the selective mechanism. They support selection acting on the inversion as an integrated genomic unit rather than on a single identifiable locus within it. The region is gene-poor, lacks functional enrichment, contains thousands of highly differentiated variants distributed across the block, and shows no evidence of a localized selective sweep. Together, these features are consistent with long-term selection on the alternative chromosomal arrangements as supergenes \citep{kirkpatrick2010,berdan2023}.

Two methodological lessons from this analysis may be broadly useful for inversion studies. First, estimates of inversion age can be based on divergence between the two arrangements, which is symmetric with respect to their ancestral and derived states. The age estimate therefore remains informative even when polarization is initially incorrect, as occurred here because the \illex{} reference genome itself carries the derived inverted arrangement. More generally, assuming that the reference orientation is ancestral creates a circularity in inversion polarization. Using an outgroup, particularly at sites where the outgroup does not simply match the reference allele, provides a way to establish arrangement ancestry independently of the reference sequence.

Second, the genealogy of a segregating inversion is not adequately represented by treating the alternative arrangements as freely recombining chromosomes or as allelic states at a single locus. Suppressed recombination between arrangements instead creates a structured coalescent resembling two partially isolated demes separated by a recombination barrier \citep{navarro2000,guerrero2012}. Modeling this history therefore requires the barrier to be represented explicitly in the ancestral recombination graph. The \texttt{msinv} simulator implements this process, allowing the age and frequency history of the inversion to be fitted jointly from within-arrangement diversity, divergence between arrangements, and the per-arrangement site-frequency spectrum. In \illex{}, this framework resolves not only the age of the inversion but also its prolonged high-frequency phase and subsequent decline, features that would be lost by reducing the inversion to a single-locus polymorphism. As genomic surveys identify increasing numbers of segregating inversions in natural populations \citep{wellenreuther2018,merot2020}, reconstructing their age and frequency trajectories may provide a useful way to distinguish among inversion histories even when their phenotypic effects are unknown.

\paragraph{Selective sweeps are enriched near neural and photoreceptive genes and are robust to background selection.}
The genome-wide sweep scan identified two prominent functional themes. The first encompasses neurogenesis, neuron development, and neuron-projection and cell morphogenesis, consistent with a coleoid lineage characterized by a large, centralized, and cell-type-rich nervous system \citep{albertin2015,styfhals2022}. Coleoid genomes also show extensive regulatory reorganization around neural genes \citep{schmidbaur2022}. A related signal has been reported in another coleoid lineage: in the sibling octopuses \textit{Octopus bimaculatus} and \textit{O.\ bimaculoides}, genes associated with brain and eye development are enriched among targets of positive selection \citep{coffing2026}. Together, these observations suggest that neural and visual functions may be recurrent targets of selection across independently evolving coleoid lineages.

The second theme involves photoreception and response to light. Cephalopod photoreception extends beyond the camera eye, with opsins and components of phototransduction pathways expressed in the skin and chromatophore system \citep{ramirez2015,kingston2015}. Their visual systems are also unusually complex, including highly differentiated optic lobes that evolved independently of the vertebrate visual system \citep{songcocasey2022,styfhals2022,yoshida2015}. In \illex{}, this signal has a plausible ecological context. The species is a visually guided predator that undergoes diel vertical migration through strong gradients in illumination and large seasonal movements across latitude \citep{odor1998}. These observations do not identify the selective pressure responsible for individual sweeps, but they provide a biological context for the repeated association of sweep candidates with light-response genes.

A central concern is whether these enrichments could arise from background selection rather than recent positive selection. Diversity is reduced in gene-dense regions of the \illex{} genome, as expected under background selection \citep{charlesworth1993,mcvicker2009,charlesworth2021}, and neural-development genes could therefore be over-represented among low-diversity windows even in the absence of sweeps. We addressed this directly by requiring candidate regions to show a reduction in diversity beyond that expected from local coding density. Rather than weakening after this filter, the major functional signals became stronger: enrichment for response to light increased from $1.9$- to $3.2$-fold, response to radiation from $1.8$- to $2.8$-fold, and morphogenesis-related terms from approximately $1.7$--$1.8$- to $2.2$--$2.3$-fold. Light response, initially the weaker of the two major themes, became the strongest after the background-selection screen. Thus, the neural and photoreceptive enrichments cannot be readily explained by gene density and its associated reduction in neutral diversity alone.

We nevertheless treat the individual sweep loci as candidates rather than confirmed targets of positive selection. Simulation-trained window classifiers such as diploS/HIC provide a powerful framework for detecting recent selection \citep{schrider2016,kern2018diploshic}, but their absolute classification proved poorly calibrated in this genome. Although the simulated data reproduced the fitted demography, empirical sample size, and missing-data structure, putatively neutral genomic windows contained substantially more fine-scale heterogeneity in diversity than the simulations. This mismatch caused widespread classification of real genomic windows as linked to sweeps. We therefore did not interpret the raw classifier output directly. Instead, we ranked sweep scores empirically within strata of callable sequence and coding density, required spatial coherence with a recombination-aware decoder, and used independent RAiSD or SweepFinder2 support to define the most conservative candidate set. An additional background-selection screen then tested whether local diversity was lower than expected from coding density. This layered approach follows the broader principle that selection scans should explicitly accommodate genomic features that cannot be reproduced adequately by the null model \citep{johri2022}.

Hard-sweep classifications predominate among the retained candidates, which is potentially interesting because large populations with abundant standing variation are often expected to provide favorable conditions for soft sweeps \citep{messer2013soft}. Hard sweeps can instead arise when adaptation depends on new or initially rare variants, particularly when the mutational target is limited \citep{hermisson2005,harris2018}. However, we do not regard the apparent excess of hard sweeps as strong evidence for a particular mode of adaptation. The soft-sweep class is less well resolved in these unphased data, and the imperfect calibration of the classifier further limits inference about the relative prevalence of hard and soft sweeps. The more robust conclusion is therefore the localization and functional enrichment of candidate sweep regions, rather than their assignment to a specific sweep class.

\paragraph{Demography, sweepstakes reproduction, and the interpretation of selection.}
The demographic history of \illex{} is important not only as a description of population history, but also because it determines the genealogical null against which both the inversion and the sweep scan are interpreted. Genome-wide Tajima's $D$ is approximately $-2$ across nearly all of the accessible genome, and the folded SFS is consistent with a major historical expansion. The fitted moments model places the onset of this expansion at approximately 770{,}000 years ago, with $\Ne$ increasing from about $5.5\times10^5$ to $6.8\times10^6$, a roughly 12-fold increase. Stairway Plot 2 and GONE2 recover the same qualitative history and similarly large contemporary effective population sizes. For an annual, highly fecund pelagic species, this combination of a large $\Ne$, abundant rare variation, and very weak geographic differentiation is consistent with a population that has remained both large and highly connected over much of its recent evolutionary history.

High fecundity alone, however, makes this interpretation non-trivial. Marine broadcast spawners are canonical candidates for sweepstakes reproductive success, in which a small and variable fraction of adults contributes disproportionately to subsequent generations \citep{eldon2006,hedgecock2011}. Under sufficiently extreme reproductive skew, genealogies can depart from the binary-merger Kingman coalescent and instead approach a multiple-merger coalescent, producing an excess of rare variants that can resemble demographic expansion. Recurrent selective sweeps can generate related distortions; in Atlantic cod, pervasive linked selection has been proposed to contribute to multiple-merger-like genealogies \citep{arnason2023}. Thus, the strongly negative Tajima's $D$ in \illex{} cannot by itself distinguish population growth from reproductive or selective processes that reshape genealogies.

We therefore tested the coalescent class directly rather than assuming that the life history of \illex{} implied a multiple-merger process. The folded SFS is substantially better fit by Kingman coalescence with population growth than by any of the Beta-coalescent models examined, and contemporary $\Ne$ inferred independently from the SFS and linkage disequilibrium is mutually consistent under the growth model. These results do not imply that reproductive success is equal among individuals, nor do they exclude sweepstakes reproduction in individual cohorts. Rather, they indicate that reproductive skew is not sufficiently strong or persistent to dominate the genome-wide genealogical process at the temporal and genomic scales represented by these data. Despite its extreme fecundity and semelparous life history, \illex{} therefore does not show the genome-wide multiple-merger signature that might otherwise be expected for a marine broadcast spawner.

This distinction has direct consequences for the evolutionary inferences in this study. First, it supports the use of a Kingman-based demographic history for estimating the age and frequency trajectory of the chromosome-2 inversion. Second, it provides a demographic null for the sweep scan in which the genome-wide excess of rare variants is attributed primarily to population growth rather than being treated as evidence of pervasive positive selection. The strong expansion also helps explain why raw sweep classifiers trained under simpler neutral models performed poorly: the baseline genome is already strongly displaced toward rare variants, leaving recent selection to be identified as a local departure from an unusually non-equilibrium genomic background. Establishing the coalescent and demographic context is therefore a prerequisite for interpreting both forms of selection described here.

The timing of the expansion also places the chromosome-2 inversion in a particularly informative historical context. The inferred origin of the inversion, approximately 800{,}000--850{,}000 years ago, overlaps the estimated onset of population expansion at approximately 770{,}000 years ago. The derived arrangement therefore appears to have arisen near the beginning of the demographic episode that generated much of the standing variation observed today. Yet its subsequent history differs sharply from the genome-wide demographic trajectory. While effective population size increased by roughly an order of magnitude, the inversion remained near a frequency of $p\approx0.70$--0.74 for much of its history before declining to its present frequency of 0.374. Population growth alone therefore cannot explain the inversion trajectory: the arrangement remained at high frequency across a profound change in population size and declined only much later. This temporal separation reinforces the inference that the inversion's history reflects selection acting on the arrangement rather than a passive response to the demographic expansion.

\section{Limitations}
Several uncertainties limit the resolution, rather than the overall interpretation, of our results. Most importantly, the callset is unphased, preventing the use of haplotype-homozygosity statistics and reducing our ability to distinguish hard from soft sweeps. The simulation-trained classifier also remains imperfectly calibrated to the empirical genome because real genomic windows contain substantially more fine-scale diversity heterogeneity than the simulated null. Our empirical stratification, spatial filtering, cross-method corroboration, and background-selection screen reduce sensitivity to this mismatch but do not provide an absolute false-positive rate. We therefore place greater confidence in the recurrent functional enrichment and in candidates supported by multiple lines of evidence than in the classification of individual loci or their assignment to a particular sweep mode.

The inversion analysis has different limitations. Its absolute age depends on the assumed mutation rate, and the fitted frequency history assumes a deterministic trajectory with neutrality within arrangements. The data therefore constrain when the arrangements diverged and how their relative frequencies changed, but not the selective mechanism responsible for their long-term maintenance. The drift bounds also assume a panmictic history. Structured neutral histories cannot be excluded in principle, and the most obvious alternative is an allopatric origin followed by secondary contact. That history does not remove the need for selection, because the new inversion would still have to reach high frequency by drift within its isolate. In an isolate of effective size $N_B$, a new neutral inversion takes about $2N_B$ generations to reach the inferred peak frequency and about $4N_B$ generations to fix, and the isolation cannot have lasted much longer than the $\sim$800--850-ky coalescent age of the arrangements. In an isolate as large as the ancestral population ($\sim5\times10^{5}$), drift is too slow to carry the inversion to high frequency in that time, so selection within the isolate would be required. Drift is fast enough only if the isolate was small ($N_B\lesssim2\times10^{5}$), but a small isolate holds little diversity, so BB would have emerged less diverse than AA, whereas it is 1.36 times more diverse. In particular, the present analyses cannot distinguish among associative overdominance, frequency-dependent selection, or temporally varying selection, nor can they identify a phenotype associated with the alternative arrangements. Comparative sampling of the inversion in closely related \textit{Illex} species and direct phenotypic comparison among karyotypes would provide important tests of these alternatives.

Finally, functional annotation remains imperfectly resolved. Functional interpretation relies heavily on homology to relatively distant cephalopod models, leaving many lineage-specific genes poorly characterized. The neural and photoreceptive signals should therefore be interpreted primarily as gene-set-level enrichments, while individual candidate genes remain hypotheses for functional validation.

\section{Conclusions}

The population genome of \illex{} is strikingly homogeneous geographically but not evolutionarily. A conspecific chromosome-level reference reveals genomic variation that was largely inaccessible when population structure was viewed through a divergent reference and geographic differentiation alone. Most notably, a large chromosome-2 inversion persists across an otherwise panmictic population as a deeply differentiated, long-lived polymorphism whose history is inconsistent with neutral drift. Its lack of a geographic cline or obvious functional specialization makes it distinct from many classic inversion systems and emphasizes that the evolutionary history of a supergene can sometimes be reconstructed more clearly than its phenotype or selective mechanism.

Beyond the inversion, the genome records a different mode of selection in localized candidate sweep regions enriched near genes involved in neural development and photoreception. Together, these results show that extensive dispersal and negligible geographic structure do not imply an absence of adaptive genomic variation. Instead, selection in a highly connected marine population can be expressed through persistent structural polymorphism and localized genomic change without producing conventional population subdivision. More broadly, the study illustrates how appropriate reference genomes and analyses that explicitly account for demography, recombination, and genomic heterogeneity can expose evolutionary variation that otherwise remains cryptic.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
The main steps are summarised here. Parameter values, thresholds, and software versions are given in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table~S1. The scripts for every step are in the \texttt{pipeline/} directory of the manuscript repository, and the inversion analysis is in the msinv repository (Data and code availability).

\paragraph{Samples and variant calling.}
We analysed all 633 \illex{} individuals whole-genome sequenced by \citet{baker2025} (ENA project PRJEB101095),
collected in 2022 and 2023 across eleven NAFO divisions, with coordinates, date, and capture depth recorded for
each collection event (Supplementary Table~S8). That study analyzed 540 of them after removing individuals with
more than 25\% missing genotypes, a consequence of mapping to the distantly related \textit{Thysanoteuthis}
reference. We re-processed the raw reads from scratch against the conspecific \texttt{Illex\_F24} primary
assembly (45 numbered chromosomes plus the Z), and against this reference no individual needed to be excluded.
Reads were trimmed with fastp \citep{chen2018fastp}, mapped with BWA-MEM2 \citep{vasimuddin2019bwamem2}, and
deduplicated with Picard, and genotypes were called jointly across individuals with GATK HaplotypeCaller in gVCF
mode \citep{poplin2018gatk}, all within grenepipe \citep{czech2022grenepipe}. The joint callset retains invariant
sites. Variants were filtered on site quality, mapping quality, strand bias, missingness, and excess
heterozygosity at intermediate frequency, and sites in GC-corrected high-depth windows or in annotated repeats were excluded, leaving 1.43~Gb of accessible sequence, of which 96\% is uniquely mappable by 150-bp reads according to GenMap \citep{pockrandt2020genmap}. For the sweep scan and gene-set analyses this accessibility mask was extended by the 22 anomalous-diversity blocks described in the Results (Supplementary Table~S9). Genotypes are unphased.

\paragraph{Diversity, site-frequency spectra, and demography.}
Mean sequencing depth is about 9-fold, so diversity statistics were estimated from genotype likelihoods rather
than from called genotypes. Folded site-frequency spectra, $\pi$, Watterson's $\theta$, and Tajima's $D$ were
computed with ANGSD \citep{korneliussen2014angsd} directly from the BAM alignments rather than from the VCF, genome-wide, per chromosome, per
NAFO division, and per karyotype, with the Z analyzed in males only. Demographic history was inferred with
Stairway~Plot~2 \citep{liu2020stairway}, moments \citep{jouganous2017moments}, and GONE2 \citep{gone2}, and the
fitted model was validated by forward simulation. The coalescent class was tested by comparing the folded
spectrum with Kingman-plus-growth and Beta-coalescent expectations. Statistics that require individual genotypes
were computed on the called genotypes with pg\_gpu \citep{pggpu}. These are the windowed per-arrangement $\pi$ and $\dxy$, the between-karyotype and between-division Hudson $\Fst$ \citep{bhatia2013}, linkage disequilibrium, and the principal-component analyses, with missing genotypes retained and handled pairwise. Per-SNP geographic $\Fst$ was computed with plink2 \citep{chang2015plink2} using the Weir--Cockerham estimator. Relatedness was screened with KING-robust \citep{manichaikul2010}.

\paragraph{The chromosome-2 inversion.}
Karyotypes were assigned by local PCA \citep{li2019lostruct} and a Gaussian-mixture model on the first principal
component. The arrangements were polarised from an AnchorWave \citep{song2022anchorwave} whole-genome alignment
to \coin{}, using only sites at which the outgroup does not simply match the reference, with est-sfs
\citep{keightley2018estsfs} as a check. The age and frequency trajectory were fitted with msinv \citep{msinv}, a sequentially Markov coalescent simulator that enforces the recombination barrier between arrangements
(Supplementary Methods). Gene content, fixed differences between arrangements, gene-ontology enrichment against a
permutation null, the drift bounds, and the mutational-load test are described in the Supplementary Methods.

\paragraph{Recombination.}
A recombination map was estimated with ReLERNN \citep{adrion2020relernn}, which trains a recurrent neural network
on coalescent simulations matched to the fitted demography and the accessibility mask and then predicts the rate
from linkage disequilibrium in windows of about 19~kb. The map was built from the 329 confidently sexed males
that had calls on every chromosome, one of 330 being removed, and a bootstrap correction was applied.

\paragraph{Selective sweeps.}
We classified 100-kb windows with diploS/HIC \citep{kern2018diploshic}, trained on SLiM forward simulations
\citep{haller2019slim} recapitated with msprime \citep{kelleher2018,haller2019recap} and on neutral msprime
simulations under the fitted demography, at the empirical sample size and with the empirical pattern of missing
data. Training and scanning used the same unphased feature extractor and the full site-frequency spectrum. Each
window's sweep score was ranked within strata of callable fraction and coding density, and outliers were
corroborated with RAiSD \citep{alachiotis2018raisd} and SweepFinder2 \citep{degiorgio2016}. A
recombination-aware hidden Markov model resolved the window classifications into spatially coherent Tier-1
calls. The Z was scanned in males only with a model retrained at their sample size. The extended mask excluded the 22 anomalous-diversity blocks throughout. Candidate genes were annotated with EnTAP
\citep{hart2020entap} and checked against cephalopod proteomes with DIAMOND \citep{buchfink2021diamond}.
Gene-ontology enrichment used Fisher's exact test with Benjamini--Hochberg correction \citep{benjamini1995}, and
the background-selection screen retained calls whose local diversity lies at least 0.003 below the value expected from coding density within 1~Mb (Supplementary Methods).

\paragraph{McDonald--Kreitman tests.}
McDonald--Kreitman tests used mkado \citep{mkado} with \coin{} as the outgroup for 20{,}999 genes, with codon
degeneracy annotated by degenotate \citep{degenotate}.
